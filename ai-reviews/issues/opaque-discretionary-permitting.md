# Issue Brief: Opaque Discretionary Permitting and Waivers

**Status:** Priority example · research brief (not a scored bill)  
**Domain:** Cross-cutting federal and state administrative law (infrastructure, energy, environment, land use)  
**Last updated:** 2026-08-02

---

## Issue description

Major projects—transmission lines, manufacturing plants, water infrastructure, broadband—often die in process: serial reviews, conflicting agencies, and decisions that turn on who can wait and who can lobby. Rules that sound protective become permission systems without clear yes/no criteria, so outcomes look arbitrary and favor the connected.

## How government blocks free-market solutions

A free market can price risk and insure harm; it cannot price an unbounded veto. When law grants agencies power to approve, waive, or stall “in the public interest” or “as appropriate” without published objective criteria and deadlines, government—not consumer demand—becomes the binding constraint on building and competition.

## CFMI proposed approach

Educational and research work only—not whipping votes or advising any person how to act:

1. **Score** permitting-reform and environmental-process bills for real constraint versus new discretion.  
2. **Publish conflicts** on waiver language, silent vetoes, and non-published criteria.  
3. **Offer open fix / alignment language** — default timelines, deemed approval/denial, single lead agency with bounded scope, public decision records.  
4. **Circulate sample model text** for bipartisan review and comment: procedural clarity that does not create new subsidies or named privileges.

## Success looks like

Permitting turns on published criteria and clocks, not who can lobby longest; reform bills that merely reshuffle opaque waivers are Hard-Flagged with open alignment language for bipartisan comment.

---

### Charter fit (summary)

Opaque discretionary waivers as artificial manipulation; radical transparency; least coercion via clear rights and shot clocks; rent surface of case-by-case authority.

### Hooks to flag

- “May waive,” “for good cause,” “as appropriate,” or “in the public interest” without published standards.  
- Unlimited or restartable review clocks; failure-to-act with no legal consequence.  
- Confidential settlements or unpublished exceptions available only to applicants with counsel access.  
- Layered vetoes where each agency can kill a project after others have approved, with no coordination duty or deadline.
