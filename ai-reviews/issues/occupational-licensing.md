# Issue Brief: Occupational Licensing Barriers

**Status:** Priority example · research brief (not a scored bill)  
**Domain:** State (primarily) · some federal spillover via accreditation and interstate practice  
**Slug:** `occupational-licensing`  
**Last updated:** 2026-08-02

Educational research only—not legal advice, voting instructions, or counsel to any person.

---

## Issue at a glance

Many people cannot lawfully offer ordinary services—hair braiding, home remodeling, interior design, aspects of healthcare, and dozens of other trades—until they complete hundreds or thousands of hours of schooling, pay fees, and obtain a state license. The usual public rationale is health and safety. A large body of economic and competition-policy research finds that licensing often functions as an entry barrier: it shelters incumbents, reduces who may compete, raises prices for consumers, and slows interstate mobility—frequently with little clear gain in measured service quality ([White House CEA / Treasury / DOL, 2015](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/default/files/docs/licensing_report_final_nonembargo.pdf); [FTC Economic Liberty advocacy](https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/advocacy/economic-liberty/selected-advocacy-relating-occupational-licensing)).

Safety-sensitive work still needs clear, evidence-based rules. The Charter question is whether a political permission slip—often written with heavy incumbent input—is the least-coercive tool that actually protects the public.

---

## How government blocks free-market solutions

The barrier is not “the market refusing to compete.” Statute and board regulation make open entry illegal without a government permission slip. Boards are often dominated by current licensees who set hour requirements, exam content, and scope lines that raise rivals’ costs. That pattern—public-interest language with incumbent-favorable design—is what economists and competition agencies flag as capture risk, not a conspiracy theory.

Where licensing is the only lawful path, voluntary certification, private insurance, reputation, and liability rules cannot fully substitute. Consumers then face thinner competition and, on the evidence reviewed by the CEA and others, prices that are often several percent higher. Workers with skill but without the approved credential stay out—or, in some trades and healthcare scopes, must route work through a licensed gatekeeper rather than serve customers directly.

## Voices & evidence — Supporting reform

| Type | Source | What it shows |
|------|--------|----------------|
| Expert / agency | [Occupational Licensing: A Framework for Policymakers](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/default/files/docs/licensing_report_final_nonembargo.pdf) (Treasury, CEA, DOL, July 2015) | Licensing share of workforce rose sharply; can protect health/safety when careful, but often raises prices, restricts employment, and impedes interstate mobility; certification may be a better balance in many cases. |
| Expert / agency | [FTC: Selected Advocacy Relating to Occupational Licensing](https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/advocacy/economic-liberty/selected-advocacy-relating-occupational-licensing) | Long record of staff comments urging states to use the least restrictive regulation that addresses substantiated consumer harm. |
| Expert / agency | [FTC prepared statement on competition and occupational licensure](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/1253073/house_testimony_licensing_and_rbi_act_sept_2017_vote.pdf) (House Judiciary, Sept. 2017) | Framework: adverse competition effects? targeted to actual harm? narrowly tailored / less restrictive alternatives? |
| Expert / research | [Johnson & Kleiner, NBER w24107](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24107) — *Is Occupational Licensing a Barrier to Interstate Migration?* | State-specific licensing exams associated with ~36% lower interstate migration vs. other occupations; national-exam fields show no such penalty. |
| Expert / research | [Institute for Justice, *Too Many Licenses?*](https://ij.org/report/too-many-licenses/) | Across hundreds of state sunrise reviews, ~80% decline to recommend new licensure; lobbies—not consumer harm—often initiate proposals. |
| Affected people *(IJ client story)* | [Arkansas hair braiding — Nivea Earl & Christine McLean](https://ij.org/case/arkansas-hair-braiding/) | Braiders faced 1,500-hour cosmetology mandates unrelated to braiding; fines and shutdown risk for serving customers without a permission slip. |
| Affected people *(IJ initiative overview)* | [IJ braiding / economic liberty](https://ij.org/issues/economic-liberty/braiding/) | Documents braiders and similar entrepreneurs blocked by cosmetology-style hour and tuition barriers; tracks state-by-state reform. |

Key findings in plain language:

- **Prices and entry.** The 2015 CEA framework summarizes evidence that licensing often raises prices and reduces employment opportunities relative to less restrictive regulation.  
- **Mobility.** Johnson & Kleiner (NBER) tie state-specific exams to lower interstate moves—workers cannot take skills across state lines without re-licensure friction.  
- **Sunrise skepticism.** Independent government sunrise reviews usually reject new licenses when asked to show public harm first (IJ archive of state reviews).  
- **Lived barriers.** Hair-braiding cases show how “safety” framing can force irrelevant training costs onto people already serving willing customers.

## Voices & evidence — Counterarguments / defense of status quo

**Steelman:** Licensing can be a response to asymmetric information—buyers cannot easily judge quality before harm occurs—and a tool for minimum competence, title clarity, and post-entry discipline in high-stakes fields.

| Type | Source | Steelman claim |
|------|--------|----------------|
| Theory | [Leland, “Quacks, Lemons, and Licensing” (JPE 1979)](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/260838) | With asymmetric quality information, markets can undersupply quality; minimum quality standards / licensing can raise welfare in some cases (though profession-set standards tend to run too high). |
| Research (nuanced) | [Kleiner & Soltas, NBER w26383](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26383) — *A Welfare Analysis of Occupational Licensing in U.S. States* | Licensing can raise consumer willingness to pay (quality/selection); higher WTP offsets ~80% of price increases for consumers—even while the paper still estimates average welfare losses. |
| Professional association | [ANA: Nurse Title Protection](https://www.nursingworld.org/practice-policy/advocacy/state/title-nurse-protection/) | Restricting the title “nurse” to licensed practitioners protects the public from incompetent or unscrupulous practice; licensure + practice acts set entry qualifications and conduct standards. |
| Board / regulator | [FSMB: Guide to Medical Regulation — Introduction](https://www.fsmb.org/u.s.-medical-regulatory-trends-and-actions/guide-to-medical-regulation-in-the-united-states/introduction/) | State medical boards exist to protect public health, safety, and welfare through licensing and discipline of physicians and related practitioners. |
| Consumer-protection logic (same CEA report) | [CEA 2015 framework](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/default/files/docs/licensing_report_final_nonembargo.pdf) | Careful licensing *can* offer important health and safety protections; the policy failure is overbreadth and poor cost–benefit design, not the idea that some fields need competence gates. |

Honest counterarguments CFMI must answer (not dismiss):

1. **Information failure.** Lay buyers of surgery, anesthesia, or structural electrical work may not detect low quality until catastrophic harm—reputation alone may be too slow.  
2. **Discipline and removal.** Licensure creates a roster and a pathway to suspend bad actors; voluntary certification lacks the same statewide stop-work power.  
3. **Title confusion.** Unrestricted use of “nurse,” “physician,” or similar titles can mislead patients about training (ANA title-protection rationale).  
4. **Quality / WTP.** Some empirical work finds consumers value licensed labor enough to offset much of the price bump (Kleiner & Soltas)—so “prices up, quality unchanged” is not always the full story.  
5. **Narrow vs. broad.** Defenders often concede braiding or floristry overreach while insisting medicine, nursing, and high-voltage trades still need licenses.

---

## CFMI response

1. **What we accept.** Asymmetric information and irreversible harm are real in some fields. Narrow, evidence-tied competence rules, clear titles, and post-harm remedies can be consistent with free markets when they are the least-coercive tool that works. The CEA’s own “careful licensing can protect” clause is not a blank check for every occupation.

2. **What we reject or narrow.** Blanket hour mandates unrelated to demonstrated risk; boards controlled by incumbents with vague “public interest” denial power; scope rules that force capable workers through licensed intermediaries without a safety case; and new licenses that sunrise reviews reject. Capture and artificial barriers are Hard-Flag territory under the Charter.

3. **Least-coercive path.** Prefer: published objective criteria → voluntary or state certification → bonding / insurance → targeted practice rules (sanitation, building codes) → full licensure only where harm is substantiated and alternatives fail. Interstate recognition with objective criteria beats re-doing thousands of hours. Sunsets and public-member majorities on boards reduce capture.

4. **Bipartisan comment.** Reform language should be written so safety-minded Democrats and opportunity-minded Republicans can both see their legitimate concerns addressed—without buying coalition support with new rents or opaque discretion.

---

## Proposed approach

Educational and research work only—not whipping votes or advising any person how to act:

1. **Score living bills** that expand or “modernize” licensing (new titles, more hours, title protection, interstate compact language that entrenches boards).  
2. **Publish conflicts** where text hardens barriers, expands board discretion, creates transferable privileges for licensees, or routes practice through opaque supervision gates without a clear safety case.  
3. **Offer open fix / alignment language** — sunsets, hour caps, right-to-earn defaults, recognition of out-of-state licenses with objective criteria, and narrower alternatives (certification, bonding, targeted health/safety rules) where evidence does not support a full license.  
4. **Circulate sample model text** for bipartisan review and comment (queued: occupational licensing reform)—least-coercive, anti-capture, no new rents.

## What would make reform passable

Addressing legitimate counterarguments so reforms survive scrutiny and still meet Charter filters:

| Legitimate concern | Design response |
|--------------------|-----------------|
| High-stakes harm (medicine, structural trades) | Keep narrow licenses; require sunrise-style harm evidence before *new* licenses; do not treat florists and surgeons as one category. |
| “Who polices bad actors?” | Pair barrier cuts with clear civil remedies, disclosure, and—where a license remains—discipline standards that are published and objective, not vague moral-character vetoes. |
| Title deception | Allow narrow title protection for clearly defined high-stakes titles while opening practice rights (or certification) for adjacent work that does not imply the same credential. |
| Quality / WTP evidence | Demand occupation-specific outcome data; if quality gains are real, prefer certification or competency exams over hour inflation and incumbent-majority boards. |
| Interstate confusion | Reciprocity / recognition with objective “substantially equivalent” criteria published in advance—not board discretion as a second veto. |
| Sunrise / lobby imbalance | Default against new licenses when independent review finds no substantiated harm; publish who requested the license. |

Passability never outranks the Charter: no subsidies, entry barriers for rivals, opaque discretion, or named privileges as the price of a coalition.

### Charter fit (summary)

Free markets and open entry; opposition to barriers that raise rivals’ costs; least-coercive tools first; competitive federalism (no federal commandeering of state boards); radical transparency on who seeks new licenses.

### Hooks to flag

- “No person shall engage in [occupation] without a license issued by the Board…”  
- Hour or credit mandates far above demonstrated safety need; “substantially equivalent” left undefined.  
- Board composition requiring a majority of current licensees with no public-member majority or sunset.  
- Vague “public interest,” “moral character,” or “as the Board deems appropriate” denial authority without published objective criteria.  
- Supervision / collaborative-practice mandates that function as incumbent vetoes over who may serve patients or clients.
