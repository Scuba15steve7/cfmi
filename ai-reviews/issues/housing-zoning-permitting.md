# Issue Brief: Housing Zoning and Permitting Barriers

**Status:** Priority example · research brief (not a scored bill)  
**Domain:** Local and state land-use law; federal funding conditions sometimes amplify  
**Slug:** `housing-zoning-permitting`  
**Last updated:** 2026-08-02

Educational research only—not legal advice, voting instructions, or counsel to any person.

---

## Issue at a glance

In many U.S. metros, housing prices sit far above the physical cost of building because law and process choke supply. Willing buyers, renters, and builders exist; zoning maps, discretionary review, parking and lot minimums, fee stacks, and multi-year permits make the deal illegal, uneconomic, or politically allocated. The usual public narratives—neighborhood character, infrastructure capacity, environmental protection, and “affordable housing”—sometimes name real externalities. Often they also bury operative barriers that freeze density, raise rivals’ costs, and protect scarcity rents for incumbents.

This is not a free-market failure of “builders refusing to build.” It is political control of use, density, and process. Progressive YIMBY supply evidence and conservative property-rights critiques often point at the same artificial scarcity—even when they disagree on remedies.

---

## How government blocks free-market solutions

Open entry into residential construction is blocked by a stack of rules, not a single talking point:

- **Use and density maps** that ban duplexes, townhomes, or apartments on most residential lots, often with large minimum lot sizes.  
- **Discretionary gates** — conditional use, special permits, design review — with soft standards (“character,” “compatibility,” “harmony”) that function as neighbor vetoes.  
- **Process as product** — completeness letters, sequential agency reviews, and no deemed-approved shot clock turn delay into a soft kill after sunk cost.  
- **Quantitative floors** — parking minimums, setbacks, and unit caps that shrink what a lot can hold without saying “no apartments.”  
- **Money gates** — impact fees, exactions, and inclusionary set-asides or in-lieu taxes that raise the marginal cost of every new unit.  
- **Overlays** — historic districts, design overlays, HOA covenants, and environmental review regimes that add another veto layer on top of the base map.

Where demand is high and supply is legally constrained, prices absorb the wedge. That pattern is the Charter problem: barriers, opaque discretion, and incumbent shelter—not missing markets.

## Hidden barriers / buried mechanisms

**Public narrative vs. operative mechanism.** Hearings feature character, schools, traffic, trees, and affordability. The operative rulebook is often lot-size floors, single-family-only districts, discretionary design review, permit timelines without clocks, parking ratios, fee schedules, overlay freezes, and project-specific waiver markets. CFMI digs for the second layer (METHODOLOGY §4.7).

| Public narrative | Operative mechanism | Who benefits from status quo | Evidence standard |
|------------------|---------------------|------------------------------|-------------------|
| “Protect neighborhood character” | Single-family-only maps; exclusionary lot sizes; design review with subjective “harmony” standards | Incumbent homeowners (scarcity capitalization); local officials responsive to homevoters | Zoning map + text; denial findings that cite character without objective metrics |
| “Infrastructure / schools can’t handle growth” | Concurrency traps, growth moratoria, impact fees sized as exclusion rather than nexus-tied capacity | Existing residents who already have capacity; agencies collecting fees | Capital plans, fee schedules, moratorium ordinances, permit denial timelines |
| “Environmental review protects communities” | Process and litigation risk that raise rivals’ costs on infill as well as greenfield (state-aware; e.g. CEQA-style regimes where they exist) | Project opponents who win by delay; incumbents sheltered from new supply | EIR/EA timelines; litigation rates and duration; approval clocks if any |
| “Affordable housing requirements help the needy” | Inclusionary set-asides / in-lieu fees that tax new supply; density bonuses traded for opaque project deals | Political coalitions claiming credit for scarce deed-restricted units; incumbents facing less market competition | Ordinance text; fee tables; production vs. price outcomes |
| “Local control / home rule” | State preemption gaps: state “opens” entry; local design codes, owner-occupancy rules, or fees quietly close it | Local majorities of existing homeowners | State statute vs. local implementing ordinances; uptake data after reform |
| “Variance / planned unit development flexibility” | Opaque waiver markets: relief for connected projects, full stack for everyone else | Repeat players who navigate discretion; officials who allocate favors | Published objective by-right path absent; PUD/variance dockets |

**Steelman of official rationale.** Planners and local governments argue that zoning separates incompatible uses, that new residents should fund capacity they consume, and that environmental review surfaces real harms before irreversible build-out ([APA — What Is Planning?](https://www.planning.org/educators/whatisplanning/); [APA Policy Guide on Impact Fees](https://www.planning.org/policy/guides/adopted/impactfees.htm); [NLC — local authority / anti-preemption posture](https://www.nlc.org/initiative/empowering-local-authority-advocating-for-responsible-preemption/)). Neighborhood stability, school seats, water/sewer, and habitat are not fake concerns. The Charter question is whether subjective discretion, delay, and supply taxes are the least-coercive tools—or whether they function as exclusion dressed as stewardship.

**Who benefits from the status quo.** Homeowners in constrained jurisdictions capture scarcity in home values and have strong incentives to block competing supply ([Fischel, *The Homevoter Hypothesis*](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674015951)). Incumbent multifamily owners face less competition. Agencies and consultants earn fees from complex process. Would-be renters, first-time buyers, and small builders absorb higher prices and denied projects. Document incentives with maps, prices, and timelines—not conspiracy stories.

**Bipartisan frames that meet at artificial scarcity.** Progressive YIMBY / supply research (upzoning evidence, ADU success vs. half-measures) and conservative property-rights / anti-exaction challenges (permit conditions as coerced payments) often name the same buried mechanisms. CFMI uses both when they point at barriers; rejects either side’s ask for new rents or opaque privilege.

## Voices & evidence — Supporting reform

| Type | Source | What it shows |
|------|--------|----------------|
| Expert / research | [Glaeser & Gyourko, “The Economic Implications of Housing Supply” (JEP 2018)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.32.1.3) | Where prices far exceed production cost, the gap is a regulatory (“zoning”) tax; land-use limits dominate high-cost coastal markets. |
| Expert / research | [Glaeser & Gyourko, NBER w23833 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23833/w23833.pdf) | Cost-based approach: well-functioning markets deliver units near production cost; regulation creates the wedge. |
| Expert / research | [Hsieh & Moretti, “Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation” (AEJ: Macro 2019)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20170388) · [NBER w21154](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21154) | Tight supply in high-productivity cities misallocates labor nationally; constraints lowered aggregate U.S. growth (published estimate ~36% from 1964–2009). |
| Expert / research | [Shoup — high cost of minimum parking requirements](http://shoup.bol.ucla.edu/HighCost.pdf) · [UCLA parking-cost synthesis](https://escholarship.org/content/qt7kp8x4fq/qt7kp8x4fq.pdf) | Parking minimums raise construction cost, cut unit count, and force households to pay for spaces they may not need. |
| Expert / research | [Furman Center — effects of inclusionary zoning](https://www.furmancenter.org/publication/the-effects-of-inclusionary-zoning-on-local-housing-markets/) · [Terner Center IZ tradeoffs (2024)](https://ternercenter.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Inclusionary-Zoning-Paper-April-2024-Final.pdf) | IZ can act like a tax on development; evidence of higher prices / lower production in some markets; design and offsets matter. |
| Progressive / YIMBY | [California YIMBY — SB 9 early data](https://cayimby.org/blog/is-sb-9-working-heres-what-early-data-reveals/) · [Terner Center — SB 9 year one](https://ternercenter.berkeley.edu/research-and-policy/sb-9-turns-one-applications/) | Statewide “end of single-family zoning” produced thin uptake where local ordinances, fees, and missing streamlining undercut the statute—preemption-gap evidence. |
| Progressive / YIMBY | [CalMatters — YIMBY Law assessment of CA housing laws](https://calmatters.org/housing/2025/02/california-yimby-laws-assessment-report/) | Local ordinances and strings attached explain why some state reforms produce little housing while ADU streamlining produced more. |
| Theory / political economy | [Fischel — *The Homevoter Hypothesis* (Harvard)](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674015951) | Homeowners dominate local land-use politics to protect undiversified home equity—explains exclusion without needing a conspiracy. |
| Affected people *(property owners / small builders)* | [IJ — Seattle Mandatory Housing Affordability challenge](https://ij.org/case/seattle-housing-affordability/) | Homeowners and small builders face large “affordable housing” fees or rent-restricted unit mandates as a condition of ordinary building permits. |
| Affected people *(same case, narrative)* | [IJ — “Seattle’s Affordable Housing Ordinance Makes Housing Unaffordable”](https://ij.org/ll/seattles-affordable-housing-ordinance-makes-housingunaffordable/) | Family blocked from adding a dwelling by tens of thousands in in-lieu fees framed as affordability. |

Key findings in plain language:

- **Price–cost wedge.** In constrained metros, home prices exceed minimum profitable production cost by amounts Glaeser/Gyourko treat as a regulatory tax—not a free-land scarcity story alone.  
- **National spillovers.** Binding local supply limits in productive cities push workers elsewhere and cut aggregate growth (Hsieh/Moretti).  
- **Buried cost floors.** Parking minimums and inclusionary taxes raise the cost of every new unit while sounding like traffic management or equity.  
- **Reform theater.** State upzoning that leaves local design codes, fees, and process intact can look transformative in headlines and fail in permits (SB 9 / YIMBY assessments).  
- **Lived barriers.** Small owners hit with MHA-style conditions show how “affordable housing” politics can add barriers while claiming to help.

## Voices & evidence — Counterarguments / defense of status quo

**Steelman:** Land-use rules can separate incompatible uses, force growth to pay for capacity, protect environmental resources, and preserve local democratic control over neighborhood form. Not every delay is capture; some sites have real infrastructure or habitat constraints.

| Type | Source | Steelman claim |
|------|--------|----------------|
| Planning profession | [APA — What Is Planning?](https://www.planning.org/educators/whatisplanning/) | Zoning keeps conflicting uses apart (e.g. heavy industry next to homes) and coordinates long-term community form. |
| Planning / finance | [APA Policy Guide on Impact Fees](https://www.planning.org/policy/guides/adopted/impactfees.htm) | Properly designed impact fees, tied to comprehensive and capital plans with a rational nexus, fund infrastructure for new growth rather than stop growth. |
| Planning / growth management | [APA Policy Guide on Smart Growth](https://www.planning.org/policy/guides/adopted/smartgrowth.htm) | Prioritize existing infrastructure; new development should pay for services it requires or be consciously subsidized—argument for concurrency and directed growth. |
| Local government association | [NLC — Empowering Local Authority / responsible preemption](https://www.nlc.org/initiative/empowering-local-authority-advocating-for-responsible-preemption/) | Municipalities need autonomy to match land use to local conditions; blunt state preemption can harm tailored solutions. |
| Local government association | [NLC — partnership over preemption on housing](https://www.nlc.org/article/2026/07/17/why-cities-towns-should-celebrate-the-21st-century-road-to-housing-act/) | Cities seek federal/state partnership and flexible funding for housing and infrastructure—not top-down zoning mandates. |
| Environmental process (steelman of review) | [Senate Judiciary background — CEQA and housing production](https://sjud.senate.ca.gov/sites/sjud.senate.ca.gov/files/ceqa_and_housing_production.pdf) | Environmental review exists to analyze and mitigate impacts; litigation-rate and streamlining debates are contested—reformers and defenders disagree on how binding the constraint is. |
| Nuanced empirical | [Furman Center IZ study](https://www.furmancenter.org/publication/the-effects-of-inclusionary-zoning-on-local-housing-markets/) | Effects of inclusionary zoning vary by market; Bay Area results differed from suburban Boston—design and context matter; not every IZ program shows the same supply hit. |

Honest counterarguments CFMI must answer (not dismiss):

1. **Genuine externalities.** Traffic, stormwater, school seats, and habitat loss are real; unconstrained build-out can dump costs on neighbors.  
2. **Fiscal fairness.** Existing residents argue newcomers should fund capacity expansions rather than dilute services.  
3. **Local knowledge / federalism.** One-size state preemption can ignore floodplains, water rights, or rural infrastructure limits.  
4. **Environmental process purpose.** Review regimes can surface real harms; the abuse case (delay as weapon) does not erase every legitimate study.  
5. **Affordability politics.** Deed-restricted units help specific households even if the tax-on-supply critique is right about market-rate prices—steelman both effects.  
6. **Transition and stability.** Sudden upzoning can create windfalls, speculation, or displacement pressures that communities want to manage.

## CFMI response

1. **What we accept.** Objective health/safety building codes; published infrastructure capacity rules with clear metrics; environmental rules aimed at real externalities with timelines and finality; narrow, nexus-tied fees that fund identified capacity. Local variation on flood, fire, and water constraints can be consistent with competitive federalism.

2. **What we reject or narrow.** Soft “character” vetoes; permit processes with no shot clock; parking and lot-size floors that ban missing-middle housing by math; inclusionary or in-lieu taxes that raise the cost of all new supply while branding themselves as affordability; opaque variance markets; grandfathering that shelters incumbents only; environmental or design process used primarily to raise rivals’ costs. Those are Hard-Flag territory under the Charter (barriers, opaque discretion, rents).

3. **Least-coercive path.** Prefer: by-right missing-middle and multifamily where infrastructure metrics are met → published objective design standards (not taste panels) → deemed-approved shot clocks → parking as a market choice or capped maximums near transit → fees with strict nexus/proportionality and public schedules → sunsets on moratoria → limited state preemption that opens entry with objective criteria while leaving true safety and floodplain rules local. Prefer more housing over political rationing of scarce units.

4. **Bipartisan comment.** Sample and fix language should be readable to supply-side Democrats/YIMBYs and property-rights Republicans alike—addressing schools, water, and environment with metrics—without buying coalition support with new privileges or discretionary carve-outs.

## Proposed approach

Educational and research work only—not whipping votes or advising any person how to act:

1. **Score** state/local housing bills and zoning packages for real barrier cuts versus new discretionary layers, fee taxes, or waiver markets.  
2. **Publish conflicts** when “affordable housing,” “climate,” or “neighborhood stability” vehicles create named privileges, opaque waivers, incumbent carve-outs, or supply taxes.  
3. **Offer open fix / alignment language** — by-right missing-middle housing, shot clocks, published objective standards, parking reform, nexus-tied fees only, limited preemption that leaves state experimentation room (no federal commandeering).  
4. **Circulate sample model text** for bipartisan review and comment (queued: housing permitting modernization).

## What would make reform passable

| Legitimate concern | Design response |
|--------------------|-----------------|
| School / water / sewer capacity | Publish objective concurrency metrics and capital plans; approve by-right when metrics are met; ban indefinite moratoria without sunset. |
| Traffic / street design | Prefer priced curb parking and street standards over unit-killing off-street minimums; allow unbundled parking. |
| Environmental harm | Keep substantive standards (flood, wetland, fire); add shot clocks, document finality, and curb pretextual process litigation where state law allows. |
| Neighborhood form / property values | Replace “character” with objective bulk/height/setback rules; do not use subjective taste as a soft ban on new households. |
| Fiscal fairness | Nexus-tied, proportional impact fees with public schedules—not general revenue extraction via permits. |
| Affordability for low-income households | Prefer open supply + targeted portable aid over taxing every new unit; if set-asides remain political, pair with large by-right density and no opaque deal-making. |
| Local control / federalism | Prefer state rules that open entry with objective criteria over federal commandeering; reject local ordinances that nullify state open-entry statutes via design and fee sabotage. |
| Transition shocks | Phase-ins and clear effective dates; no permanent grandfather monopoly for incumbents. |

Passability never outranks the Charter: no subsidies for favored developers, entry barriers for rivals, opaque discretion, or named privileges as the price of a coalition.

### Charter fit (summary)

Free markets and property/contract; opposition to opaque discretion and incumbent shelter; least-coercive process and by-right rules before new housing bureaucracies; competitive federalism (state experimentation, no federal commandeering of local maps).

### Hooks to flag

- Use tables that ban multifamily or ADUs on most residential lots without an objective upzoning path.  
- Discretionary “special permit / conditional use / design review” with standards like “harmony with neighborhood character.”  
- Permit timelines with no deemed-approved shot clock; endless “completeness” loops.  
- Parking, lot-size, or unit minimums that mathematically forbid missing-middle housing.  
- Inclusionary, in-lieu, or density-bonus schemes that tax supply or trade political favors for project-specific waivers unavailable on equal, published terms.  
- Overlay / historic / HOA stacks that freeze supply after headline zoning reform.  
- Environmental or design process used as a rival-cost weapon without timelines or finality.
